\begin{abstract}
The Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW) is commonly solved under a lexicographic objective: minimize the number of vehicles ($\NV$) first, then total travel distance ($\TD$). Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS) is an established metaheuristic for this problem, and recent work adds learned control to such search. This paper presents Tri-Level Hybrid DDQN-ALNS, which adds three online-learning controllers to an ALNS core: a macro plateau controller modeled approximately as a semi-Markov decision process over aggregated features, a micro operator controller that blends a Dueling Double Deep Q-Network with operator priors through an entropy gate, and a Learned Acceptance Criterion that can accept moves Simulated Annealing rejects. The controllers start from random weights in every run. The search also uses ejection chains, set partitioning with HiGHS, and a frozen Graph Neural Network edge heatmap. We claim neither a new search principle nor a convergence guarantee. We evaluate the method under equal wall-clock budgets of 10, 60, 300 and 900\,s on 74 Solomon and Gehring-Homberger instances with five seeds per instance, against ALNS-Base, PyVRP-ILS and an adapted NeuroLKH, on the same machine and in the same blocks, counting only incumbents certified in the original problem space before the cutoff. A factorial ablation on 18 instances separates learning from heuristic components. \PENDING{two sentences with the rerun's 300\,s result against each comparator and the ablation result, including any loss.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\PARstart{T}{he} Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW) is a foundational NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem underpinning modern freight logistics, last-mile parcel delivery, and sustainable distribution networks \cite{Dantzig1959, Solomon1987}. In addition to the combinatorial explosion inherent in classical routing, VRPTW enforces hard customer time-window constraints: each vehicle route must simultaneously respect vehicle capacity limits and temporal feasibility at every stop.

Commercial freight planning often treats the objective as lexicographic: dispatchers prioritize minimizing the total number of deployed vehicles ($\NV$) before minimizing total travel distance ($\TD$) \cite{Toth2014, braysy2005vehicle}. Formally, letting $\mathcal{S}$ denote the space of feasible routing solutions, a solution $s_1 \in \mathcal{S}$ is strictly preferred over $s_2 \in \mathcal{S}$ (denoted $s_1 \prec s_2$) if and only if:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    NV(s_1) < NV(s_2) \;\lor\; \big(&NV(s_1) = NV(s_2) \;\land \\
    &TD(s_1) < TD(s_2)\big)
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:lexicographic_intro}
\end{equation}
This hierarchical ordering reflects the economic structure of commercial transportation, where fixed vehicle capital acquisition, maintenance, insurance, and driver shift wages dominate marginal fuel consumption.

Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS) \cite{pisinger2007general, Ropke2006} has long served as a leading metaheuristic paradigm for VRPTW, exploring diverse neighborhood structures via iterative ruin-and-recreate operations while strictly preserving feasibility. However, standard ALNS implementations rely on stateless roulette-wheel or multi-armed bandit mechanisms that aggregate historical reward scores across coarse segments. This design exhibits two structural weaknesses: (i)~\textit{Stateless Operator Selection}, ignoring instantaneous search state such as spatial route fragmentation, temporal slack, or capacity utilization; and (ii)~\textit{Myopic Plateau Stagnation}, lacking multi-step coordination to escape structural plateaus where vehicle elimination requires evaluating temporarily non-improving intermediate states.

In parallel, Neural Combinatorial Optimization (NCO) has emerged as a data-driven alternative, using Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) models such as Pointer Networks \cite{vinyals2015pointer}, Attention Models \cite{kool2019attention}, and constructive policies \cite{nazari2018reinforcement}. While capturing spatial regularities without manual heuristic engineering, direct application of pure end-to-end neural solvers to industrial VRPTW encounters critical bottlenecks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[(i)] Feasibility Fragility under Tight Time Windows, where autoregressive decoding deadlocks or requires delicate penalty tunings \cite{falkner2020learning, lin2021deep};
    \item[(ii)] Objective Misalignment, optimizing scalarized distance rather than strict lexicographic fleet minimization ($\NV \succ \TD$); and
    \item[(iii)] Out-of-Distribution Sensitivity, often degrading on larger scales or different customer distributions without retuning.
\end{itemize}

To bridge these paradigms, this paper presents Tri-Level Hybrid DDQN-ALNS, a learning-augmented optimization framework that embeds state-conditioned reinforcement learning directly inside the iterative search loop of ALNS. Rather than replacing the metaheuristic with a neural solver, our approach keeps the neighborhood operators of operations research and returns only solutions validated as feasible, structuring search governance into three coordinated tiers: two learned reinforcement learning tiers (Macro Plateau Governance and Micro Operator Selection) coupled with a heuristic search tier. Whether this control pays for the compute it consumes is an empirical question, and we treat it as one. 

The primary contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item We specify a control layer for ALNS in which a macro plateau controller, a micro operator controller and an acceptance model learn online from random weights in every run, with no offline policy training. Destroy-repair search, Simulated Annealing, ejection chains and set partitioning are established methods, and we do not claim a new search principle. What we study is whether such cold-start control pays for its compute within a time budget.
    \item We evaluate the method under equal wall-clock budgets of 10, 60, 300 and 900\,s on 74 benchmark instances (56 Solomon-100, 12 Homberger-200, and 6 Homberger-400) with five seeds, against ALNS-Base, PyVRP-ILS and an adapted NeuroLKH. All cases of an instance, seed and cutoff run as one block, and only incumbents certified in the original problem space before the cutoff count.
    \item We separate learning from heuristic machinery with a wall-clock factorial ablation over six components at 60 and 300\,s, including a $2\times2$ contrast of the learning and heuristic bundles.
    \item We report the limits of the method and of the evidence: where it does not help, how results depend on the budget, the method's earlier dependence on reference fleet sizes, the machine-speed variation we measured, and the way test results influenced development.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related literature. Section~\ref{sec:formulation} presents the problem formulation. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the proposed Tri-Level Hybrid DDQN-ALNS methodology. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} details the empirical benchmark results, statistical tests, and ablation analyses. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses scale-aware dynamics, analytical properties, and limitations. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\subsection{Classical Metaheuristics and ALNS for VRPTW}
\label{sec:rw_classical}

Metaheuristics have long dominated practical VRPTW optimization \cite{braysy2005vehicle}. Building on Solomon's insertion heuristics \cite{Solomon1987} and Shaw's Large Neighborhood Search \cite{shaw1998using}, Ropke and Pisinger \cite{Ropke2006, pisinger2007general, pisinger2010large} formalized Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS), wherein removal and insertion operators compete for selection via roulette-wheel probabilities updated from historical performance. For fleet minimization, Guided Ejection Search \cite{nagata2009ges} and penalty-based edge assembly memetic algorithms \cite{nagata2010srex} represent dedicated mechanisms, while matheuristic hybrids leverage elite route pools to periodically solve Set Partitioning models \cite{rochat1995probabilistic, alvarenga2007genetic, subramanian2013hybrid}. More recently, Slack Induction by String Removals \cite{christiaens2020slack} demonstrated that specialized ruin operators can rival complex frameworks.

Despite this success, standard ALNS has an intrinsic limitation: operator weights are updated as exponentially smoothed scores across coarse iteration segments, resulting in delayed, aggregated credit assignment. The selection policy is \emph{stateless}, ignoring route topology, cluster fragmentation, and time-window tightness. Turke\v{s} et al.\ \cite{turkes2021meta} found that this adaptive mechanism yields only marginal gains over non-adaptive LNS. That result leaves open whether a better selection policy would help or whether operator selection matters little, which is one reason we measure it directly.

\subsection{Neural Combinatorial Optimization and End-to-End Deep RL}
\label{sec:rw_nco}

Neural Combinatorial Optimization (NCO) learns data-driven routing policies using Pointer Networks \cite{vinyals2015pointer, bello2016neural}, reinforcement learning on dynamic customer states \cite{nazari2018reinforcement}, Attention Models \cite{kool2019attention}, and Graph Neural Networks \cite{khalil2017learning, joshi2019efficient}. Extending NCO to VRPTW \cite{falkner2020learning, lin2021deep} achieves rapid inference on small instances but faces several hurdles: (i)~\emph{Feasibility Fragility}, where hard time windows cause constructive decoding deadlocks; (ii)~\emph{Lexicographic Misalignment}, training on scalarized surrogate costs rather than the primary-secondary fleet hierarchy ($\NV \succ \TD$); (iii)~\emph{Out-of-Distribution Degradation} on larger scales or different topologies.

\subsection{Learning-Augmented Metaheuristics}
\label{sec:rw_hybrid}

To combine machine learning with operational guarantees, the ``Learning to Optimize'' paradigm \cite{bengio2021machine} embeds neural models inside metaheuristic search loops for operator selection \cite{kallestad2023general, lu2020learning}, solution perturbation \cite{chen2019learning}, and learned destroy policies \cite{hottung2020neural}. Closest to this work, Johnn et al.~\cite{johnn2023graph} formulate operator choice in ALNS as a Markov decision process solved with deep reinforcement learning and graph neural networks, and DR-ALNS~\cite{reijnen2024online} learns a policy that selects operators, adjusts parameters and controls the acceptance criterion, so learned influence on acceptance is not new. Our design differs in three respects: separate controllers for plateau regimes and operator pairs in place of one policy, an acceptance model added to Simulated Annealing by a disjunctive rule, and online learning from random weights in every run with no prior policy training for these controllers. We did not run these methods, and these differences are not evidence that our method performs better.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}
\label{sec:rw_positioning}

Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} situates Tri-Level Hybrid DDQN-ALNS within the routing literature. Our approach combines validated feasibility of returned solutions with two controllers at different timescales and a Learned Acceptance Criterion. Macro/micro decoupling addresses a fundamental credit-assignment challenge across timescales \cite{sutton1999between}: rewards for escaping multi-hundred-iteration plateaus are temporally distant from single destroy-repair decisions, which motivates separating the two decisions, although we do not compare against a single flat policy. The macro tier's semi-MDP formulation over $\Delta_{\text{seg}}=100$-iteration options isolates plateau governance from micro operator selection. Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} also highlights the operational trade-off: online network updates and MIP solves consume part of each time budget. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} measures whether this pays off.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

The Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW) is formally defined on a complete directed graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{A})$. The vertex set $\mathcal{V} = \mathcal{N} \cup \{0, n+1\}$ comprises customer nodes $\mathcal{N} = \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, origin depot node $0$, and destination depot node $n+1$. The physical depot is modeled as two separate nodes ($0$ and $n+1$) to represent route departure and return without self-loops. The arc set is $\mathcal{A} = \{(i, j) : i, j \in \mathcal{V}, i \neq j, i \neq n+1, j \neq 0\}$.

Each customer $i \in \mathcal{N}$ has a non-negative demand $q_i > 0$, service duration $s_i \ge 0$, and service time window $[e_i, l_i]$, where $e_i$ and $l_i$ denote the earliest and latest allowable service start times, respectively. Depot nodes satisfy $q_0 = q_{n+1} = 0$, $s_0 = s_{n+1} = 0$, and $[e_0, l_{n+1}]$ defines the global planning horizon. Each arc $(i, j) \in \mathcal{A}$ has travel distance $c_{ij} \ge 0$ and travel time $t_{ij} \ge 0$. A homogeneous fleet of $K$ vehicles, denoted by $\mathcal{K} = \{1, \dots, K\}$, is stationed at the depot, with each vehicle possessing carrying capacity $Q$.

\subsection{Mathematical Model and Decision Variables}
The optimization model employs binary routing variables $x_{ij}^k \in \{0, 1\}$ ($1$ if vehicle $k \in \mathcal{K}$ traverses arc $(i, j) \in \mathcal{A}$, and $0$ otherwise) and continuous schedule variables $w_i^k \ge 0$ specifying the time at which vehicle $k \in \mathcal{K}$ begins service at node $i \in \mathcal{V}$. The primary objective is lexicographic: first minimizing the number of deployed vehicles ($\NV$), and secondarily minimizing total travel distance ($\TD$):
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{lex}\,\min \left( \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}} x_{0j}^k, \quad \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{A}} c_{ij}\,x_{ij}^k \right)
  \label{eq:lexicographic_obj}
\end{equation}
In computational solver implementations, this hierarchy is evaluated via a scalarized surrogate objective using a large penalty $\mathcal{M} \gg \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{A}} c_{ij}$:
\begin{equation}
  \min \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \left( \mathcal{M} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}} x_{0j}^k + \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{A}} c_{ij}\,x_{ij}^k \right)
  \label{eq:scalarized_obj}
\end{equation}
subject to:
\begin{align}
  &\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{V}, j \neq i} x_{ij}^k = 1, \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{N} \label{eq:visit_once} \\
  &\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N} \cup \{n+1\}} x_{0j}^k \le 1, \quad \forall k \in \mathcal{K} \label{eq:depot_flow_depart} \\
  &\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N} \cup \{0\}} x_{j, n+1}^k = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N} \cup \{n+1\}} x_{0j}^k, \quad \forall k \in \mathcal{K} \label{eq:depot_flow_return} \\
  &\sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}, i \neq h} x_{ih}^k - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{V}, j \neq h} x_{hj}^k = 0, \quad \forall h \in \mathcal{N}, \; k \in \mathcal{K} \label{eq:flow_conservation} \\
  &\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} q_i \sum_{j \in \mathcal{V}, j \neq i} x_{ij}^k \le Q, \quad \forall k \in \mathcal{K} \label{eq:capacity_constraint} \\
  &w_i^k + s_i + t_{ij} - M_{ij} (1 - x_{ij}^k) \le w_j^k, \nonumber \\
  &\hspace{3.4cm} \forall (i, j) \in \mathcal{A}, \; k \in \mathcal{K} \label{eq:time_propagation} \\
  &e_i \le w_i^k \le l_i, \quad w_i^k \ge 0, \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{V}, \; k \in \mathcal{K} \label{eq:variable_domains} \\
  &x_{ij}^k \in \{0, 1\}, \quad \forall (i, j) \in \mathcal{A}, \; k \in \mathcal{K} \label{eq:binary_domains}
\end{align}

Constraint~\eqref{eq:visit_once} ensures that every customer is visited exactly once. Constraints~\eqref{eq:depot_flow_depart}--\eqref{eq:depot_flow_return} enforce that each vehicle departs from origin depot $0$ and arrives at destination depot $n+1$ at most once. Flow conservation across all customer nodes is guaranteed by \eqref{eq:flow_conservation}. Constraint~\eqref{eq:capacity_constraint} prevents vehicle capacity violations. Schedule propagation is maintained via \eqref{eq:time_propagation}, where $M_{ij} = \max\{0, l_i + s_i + t_{ij} - e_j\}$ defines the tightest valid Big-$M$ upper bound. Customer time-window compliance and domains are governed by \eqref{eq:variable_domains}--\eqref{eq:binary_domains}; if a vehicle arrives at customer $i$ prior to $e_i$, it incurs an idle waiting time such that service begins at $w_i^k = e_i$. While $w_i^k$ is indexed per vehicle for strict MIP formulation alignment with $x_{ij}^k$, constraint~\eqref{eq:visit_once} ensures that exactly one vehicle serves each customer $i$, rendering only one $w_i^k$ active in any feasible dispatch plan.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section details the proposed Tri-Level Hybrid DDQN-ALNS framework. The architecture integrates online adaptive reinforcement learning decision layers with operations research search mechanics to optimize the lexicographic VRPTW objective while maintaining operational constraint feasibility.

\subsection{Tri-Level Architecture Overview}
\label{sec:arch_overview}

The framework decomposes the global routing search into three coordinated operational tiers, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}. The framework orchestrates search across three coordinated tiers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Macro Decision Tier (Plateau Controller $\pi_{\text{macro}}$): formulated as an Aggregated semi-MDP over segments of length $\Delta_{\text{seg}} = 100$, where a Dueling Double DQN~\cite{mnih2015human,vanhasselt2016deep} with Prioritized Experience Replay~\cite{schaul2016prioritized} evaluates convergence velocity and transitions among 7 exploration/recombination regimes;
    \item Micro Selection Tier (Operator Controller $\pi_{\text{micro}}$ \& LAC): operates per iteration $t$, using a Micro Dueling DDQN with normalized Softmax-Entropy policy-concentration gating to select composite destroy--repair operator pairings conditioned on a 20-dimensional state vector, augmented by a Learned Acceptance Criterion (LAC) evaluating delayed downstream lookahead; and
    \item Heuristic Search Tier (Granular ALNS \& Route-Pool Recombination): executes ruin-and-recreate operations with $O(1)$-per-query Savelsbergh forward-slack insertion checks on cached route profiles, restricts moves to the $k=15$ nearest customers, and periodically solves a time-limited Set Partitioning formulation over elite routes via HiGHS under a time limit.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Macro Level: Plateau Controller (Aggregated SMDP Formulation)}
\label{sec:macro_controller}

We formulate the macro search governance tier as a Semi-Markov Decision Process\cite{sutton1999between} over an Aggregated State Representation (Aggregated SMDP) defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}_{\text{macro}}, \mathcal{A}_{\text{macro}}, P_{\text{macro}}, R_{\text{macro}}, \gamma_{\text{macro}})$. Because the underlying combinatorial route pool and fine-grained trajectory history are projected into a 12-dimensional feature space $\mathbf{s}_k^{\text{macro}}$, the decision tier operates over a compact aggregated state representation with temporal options of fixed duration $\Delta_{\text{seg}} = 100$ low-level ALNS iterations. The controller acts only once the search has stagnated for a minimum number of iterations; before that, segments run in the default mode and are not used as macro transitions. The temporally extended transition kernel $P_{\text{macro}}(\mathbf{s}_{k+1}^{\text{macro}} \mid \mathbf{s}_k^{\text{macro}}, a_k)$ denotes the distribution of the next observation after the 100 underlying stochastic destroy--repair steps. The learner discounts per macro decision with $\gamma_{\text{macro}} = 0.95$, used both in the DDQN target and in the shaping term below, rather than with $\gamma^{\Delta_{\text{seg}}}$.

The aggregated formulation is a modeling device, not a verified Markov model. The 12 features are a lossy summary of the full search state, which also includes the current routes, the route pool, the operator weights, the network parameters and the random-number state. Distinct search states therefore share an observation, and the next observation depends on components that the controller does not see, so $\mathbf{s}_k^{\text{macro}}$ is not in general a sufficient statistic and the macro process need not satisfy the Markov property. The controller is better described as a learned policy over a partially observed, state-aggregated process~\cite{li2006unified}. We provide no convergence guarantee and no bound on the approximation error. Such guarantees are not available in general for Q-learning over aggregated or partial observations, and the online DDQN additionally uses nonlinear function approximation, experience replay and target networks. The value of the controller is therefore assessed empirically through the ablation study (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}).

\subsubsection{Macro State Representation}
At the start of segment $k$, the macro controller observes a 12-dimensional state vector $\mathbf{s}_k^{\text{macro}} \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$, capturing convergence velocity, fleet economics, and solution topology:

\begin{align}
  \mathbf{s}_k^{\text{macro}} = \Big[ 
    &\min\Big(\frac{\text{no\_imp}}{\text{patience}}, 1\Big), \; \min\Big(\frac{C_{\text{cur}} - C_{\text{best}}}{C_{\text{best}}}, 1\Big), \nonumber \\
    &\min\Big(\frac{T}{T_0}, 1.5\Big), \; \text{imp\_rate}, \; \min\Big(\frac{\NVs{cur}}{\NVs{init}}, 2\Big), \nonumber \\
    &rb, \; lb, \; \tau_{\text{tight}}, \nonumber \\
    &\bar{s}, \; \rho_{\text{fill}}, \; \rho_{\text{pool}}, \; \frac{k}{K_{\max}} \Big]
  \label{eq:macro_state}
\end{align}

where $\text{no\_imp}$ is the number of consecutive unimproved iterations, $T/T_0$ is the normalized annealing temperature, $rb \in [0, 1]$ and $lb \in [0, 1]$ measure spatial route separation and capacity balance, $\tau_{\text{tight}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(l_i - e_i < 0.5 \cdot d_{\max})$ is the fraction of customers whose time-window width is below half the largest distance $d_{\max}$ of the instance, $\bar{s}$ is average forward time slack, $\rho_{\text{fill}}$ is average vehicle capacity utilization, and $\rho_{\text{pool}} = \min(|\mathcal{P}|/\mathcal{P}_{\max}, 1.0)$ tracks route pool fill ratio.

\subsubsection{Macro Action Space \& Search Regimes}
The macro action space $\mathcal{A}_{\text{macro}}$ comprises 7 search modes. Each mode scales the destroy size, multiplies the temperature once per segment, biases the operator priors and sets the local-search passes. With (destroy scale, temperature factor) in parentheses, the modes are: $a_0$ (\textsf{\small DEFAULT}, 1.00, 1.00); $a_1$ (\textsf{\small INTENSIFY}, 0.70, 0.98); $a_2$ (\textsf{\small DIVERSIFY}, 1.35, 1.08), which also permits a one-vehicle increase in the current solution; $a_3$ (\textsf{\small TW\_RESCUE}, 1.10, 1.05), with priors favoring time-window urgency removal and time-window-aware repair; $a_4$ (\textsf{\small POOL\_RECOMBINE}, 0.90, 1.01), which may trigger Set Partitioning; $a_5$ (\textsf{\small ROUTE\_REDUCE}, 0.95, 1.02), with priors favoring route-elimination operators, a Generalized Ejection Chain attempt at every iteration, penalized acceptance, and possible recombination; and $a_6$ (\textsf{\small INFEASIBLE\_DESCENT}, 1.10, 1.05), which is available only during the first search phase described below and maps to \textsf{\small DEFAULT} afterwards. The segment length is 100 iterations, or 130 on instances with wide time windows.

\subsubsection{Search Phases}
\label{sec:search_phases}
The search changes behavior over the budget. In the first phase, candidates are accepted by a penalized Simulated Annealing rule on $C_{\text{pen}}(s) = C(s) + \lambda\,(v_{\text{cap}}(s) + v_{\text{tw}}(s))$, so the current solution may be infeasible. The multiplier $\lambda$ is updated every 100 iterations, by a factor of 0.90 when more than half of recent candidates are feasible and 1.15 otherwise, and is bounded by $[5, 200]$ for instances with tight time windows and $[0.5, 50]$ for others. The phase ends after 60\% of a nominal 10,000 iterations, when the fleet reaches its floor, or, with no known floor, after 250 iterations without a new best solution (and at least 200 iterations in total). LAC is not consulted in this phase, and mode $a_5$ also keeps penalized acceptance afterwards.

The main destroy-repair loop receives $80\%$ of the time budget. The remaining $20\%$ goes to tail phases: pool recombination, polishing at fixed fleet size, dense column generation for the pool, route-elimination heuristics, and distance polishing. ALNS-Base has no tail phases. A few settings also adapt to the instance: the initial temperature is scaled by 0.8 for tight and 1.2 for wide time windows, and the plateau trigger is 1.5 times longer for $N \ge 200$.

\subsubsection{Multi-Objective Segment Reward \& Surrogate Policy Optimization}
To guide reinforcement learning exploration in alignment with the primary-secondary optimization hierarchy, the segment reward $R_{\text{seg}}$ balances vehicle reduction gains against distance savings via an adaptive fleet pressure factor $\lambda_{\text{fleet}} \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    R_{\text{seg}} = 0.30\big[&\text{Base} + \lambda_{\text{fleet}} \Psi_{\mathrm{NV}} + (1 - \lambda_{\text{fleet}}) \Psi_{\mathrm{TD}} \\
    &+ \text{Bonus}_{\mathrm{NV}}\big] + \Delta \Phi
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:segment_reward}
\end{equation}
where $\Psi_{\mathrm{NV}} = \sum_{j=1}^4 \beta_{\mathrm{NV}, j} \Delta X_j$ and \allowbreak $\Psi_{\mathrm{TD}} = \sum_{j=1}^4 \beta_{\mathrm{TD}, j} \Delta X_j$ linearly weight improvements across best-so-far and incumbent fleet size ($\Delta\NV$) and travel distance ($\Delta C$), with representative primary fleet coefficient $\beta_{\mathrm{NV}, 1} = 8.0$. Baseline computational penalty $\text{Base}$ penalizes intensive local search passes and unproductive stagnation, while $\text{Bonus}_{\mathrm{NV}}$ provides a discrete reward of $+15$ per vehicle removed from the best solution and $+5$ per vehicle removed from the current solution. All coefficients are listed in Supplementary Section~6.

The presence of two parallel fleet-reward pathways in~\eqref{eq:segment_reward} addresses credit assignment across temporal scales: (i) Dense Continuous Guidance ($\lambda_{\text{fleet}} \Psi_{\mathrm{NV}}$) encourages moves that balance inter-route capacity utilization and narrow the vehicle count gap relative to the incumbent; and (ii) Sparse Milestone Reinforcement ($\text{Bonus}_{\mathrm{NV}}$) awards these amounts only when a vehicle is eliminated, preventing the sparse route collapse signal from being diluted across routine distance variations.

\paragraph{Reward Constants}
The reward constants were set by hand during development, and Section~\ref{sec:setup} states what we can and cannot document about them and how the test set influenced later development.

We emphasize a fundamental architectural dichotomy between the combinatorial evaluation layer and the reinforcement learning training layer: (i) Strict Lexicographic Evaluation: Incumbent replacement, elite archive admission, and final solution selection strictly evaluate the true lexicographic ordering $f(s) = (\NV(s), \TD(s))$, guaranteeing that fleet reduction strictly dominates distance; and (ii) Smooth RL Reward Surrogate: The reinforcement learning policy receives scalarized reward feedback $R_{\text{seg}}$ modulated by $\lambda_{\text{fleet}}$. When surplus capacity exists ($\NVs{cur} > \NVs{best}$), setting $\lambda_{\text{fleet}} \to 1$ concentrates gradients on fleet collapse; once fleet size stabilizes ($\NVs{cur} = \NVs{best}$), setting $\lambda_{\text{fleet}} \le 0.5$ transfers gradient sensitivity to routing distance.

To accelerate learning while preventing artificial reward cycles in the surrogate MDP, we incorporate potential-based reward shaping $\Delta \Phi = \gamma_{\text{macro}}\,\Phi(\mathbf{s}_{k+1}^{\text{macro}}) - \Phi(\mathbf{s}_k^{\text{macro}})$. We explicitly note that this term implements potential-based shaping of the \emph{macro surrogate objective} \cite{ng1999policy} rather than guaranteeing policy invariance with respect to the underlying lexicographic VRPTW objective (which is enforced externally by the combinatorial evaluator). The adaptive fleet pressure factor $\lambda_{\text{fleet}} \in [0, 1]$ dynamically shifts optimization priority toward fleet reduction whenever surplus capacity exists:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\text{fleet}}(s) = \sigma\!\left(8.0 \cdot \frac{\NVs{cur} - \NVs{best}}{\NVs{init}}\right)
  \label{eq:lambda_fleet}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic sigmoid, with the state potential defined as:
\begin{align}
  \Phi(s) = &-15\,\lambda_{\text{fleet}}(s) \frac{\max(\NV - \NVs{best}, 0)}{\NVs{init}} \nonumber \\
  &- 0.18\bigl(1 - \lambda_{\text{fleet}}(s)\bigr) \nonumber \\
  &\quad \times \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\TD - \TDs{best}}{\TDs{best}} \cdot 100, -25, 25\right)
  \label{eq:potential_function}
\end{align}
The Macro Plateau Controller trains a dedicated Dueling Double DQN network ($Q_{\text{macro}}$) with prioritized replay buffer $\mathcal{D}_{\text{macro}}$, executing Adam gradient steps with Cosine Annealing learning rate scheduling at the completion of each macro segment.

\subsubsection{Composite Action Space}
The micro action space $\mathcal{A}_{\text{micro}}$ comprises $N_D \times N_R = 13 \times 5 = 65$ composite heuristic pairs, combining 13 Destroy Operators (Random, Worst-cost, Shaw spatiotemporal relatedness, Route portion removal, Time-window urgency removal, Route-Depletion Destroy, Route dispersion elimination, Costly route elimination, Cross-route Shaw, Route merge sampling, Route absorb disruption, Neural worst, Neural Shaw) with 5 Repair Operators (Greedy insertion, 2-Regret, 3-Regret, Time-window greedy, Forward-time-slack greedy).

\subsubsection{Dueling DDQN Architecture \& Policy-Concentration Blending}
The Micro Q-network adopts a Dueling architecture \cite{wang2016dueling} with layer normalization:
\begin{equation}
  Q(s, a; \theta) = V(s; \theta_v) + \Big( A(s, a; \theta_a) - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|} \sum_{a'} A(s, a'; \theta_a) \Big)
  \label{eq:dueling_q}
\end{equation}
To adaptively balance learned neural predictions with exploration priors, we implement a normalized Softmax-Entropy Policy-Concentration Gate. Let $\pi_{\theta}(a \mid s) = \operatorname{softmax}(Q(s, a)/\tau_{\text{soft}})$ be the Boltzmann policy at softmax temperature $\tau_{\text{soft}} = 1.0$. The normalized Shannon entropy is defined as:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid s)) = -\frac{1}{\ln |\mathcal{A}|} \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi_{\theta}(a \mid s) \ln \pi_{\theta}(a \mid s) \in [0, 1]
  \label{eq:policy_entropy}
\end{equation}
We define the policy-concentration weight as $w_{\text{conf}}(s) = 1.0 - \mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid s))$. High concentration ($w_{\text{conf}} \to 1$) indicates that the Boltzmann policy is sharply peaked around a dominant operator pair, whereas low concentration ($w_{\text{conf}} \to 0$) signals an exploratory regime. 

The action-selection score blends the Q-values with operator priors and the mean of a Thompson-sampling posterior through this gate, then adds a variance-aware bonus adapted from UCB \cite{auer2002finite} and UCB-V \cite{audibert2009exploration}:
\begin{align}
  Q_{\text{blend}}(s, a) = \; &w_{\text{conf}}(s)\,Q(s, a) + (1 - w_{\text{conf}}(s))\big[ \alpha_{\text{p}}\ln P(a) \nonumber \\
  &+ \beta_{\text{b}}\,\bar{\mu}_{\text{bandit}}(a) \big], \nonumber \\
  Q_{\text{eff}}(s, a) = \; &Q_{\text{blend}}(s, a) + \alpha_{\text{u}}\,\frac{U(a) - \bar{U}}{\sigma_U}, \nonumber \\
  U(a) = \; &\hat{\mu}(a) + c_{\text{ucb}}\,\sigma_a \sqrt{\ln N_t / N_a(t)},
  \label{eq:q_eff_blending}
\end{align}
where $\alpha_{\text{p}} = 0.55$ scales prior log-probabilities, $\beta_{\text{b}} = 0.20$ weights the bandit posterior mean, $\alpha_{\text{u}} = 0.35$ weights the standardized bonus over the actions, $c_{\text{ucb}} = 1.0$, $\hat{\mu}(a)$ is a running mean reward, and $\sigma_a = \sqrt{M_{2, a}/\max(N_a(t) - 1, 1)}$ is an online standard deviation tracked with Welford's algorithm. Action counters start at $0.5$ and decay by $0.993$ per step, and $N_t = \sum_a N_a(t)$.

Two properties of this rule follow directly. The Q-values and log-priors are not standardized before the gate, so the balance between them depends on the scale of the learned values. The bonus is not gated, so it continues to affect selection when the policy is concentrated.

The first 256 transitions select pairs from the bandit with the prior. After that, the controller explores with probability $\varepsilon$, sampling from the prior and bandit, with $\varepsilon$ decaying from $0.35$ to $0.02$ by a factor of $0.9996$ per step. It otherwise takes the pair that maximizes $Q_{\text{eff}}$. The Micro reward has the same form as $R_{\text{seg}}$ at iteration level, with its own coefficients and a scale of $0.45$; a rejected candidate receives a base reward of $-0.08$ before shaping.

\subsection{Learned Acceptance Criterion (LAC): SA with Predictive Lookahead Override}
\label{sec:lac}

In classical ALNS, move acceptance relies on Simulated Annealing (SA), which accepts improving transitions ($\Delta C < 0$) deterministically and non-improving transitions ($\Delta C \ge 0$) with probability $P_{\text{SA}} = \exp(-\Delta C / T)$. During deep cooling stages ($T \to 0$), $P_{\text{SA}}$ vanishes exponentially, restricting search to downhill moves and causing premature local trapping.

To reduce search entrapment during deep cooling, the Learned Acceptance Criterion (LAC) operates as an online supervised binary classifier $\phi_{\text{LAC}}: \mathbb{R}^9 \to [0, 1]$. LAC is consulted only for feasible candidates that keep the current fleet size and increase travel distance; improving, fleet-reducing and infeasible candidates follow the rules of Algorithm~\ref{alg:hybrid_ddqn_alns} unchanged, and penalty-relaxed phases use the penalized SA rule without LAC. For the consulted candidates, rather than serving as an exclusionary filter or replacement, LAC implements a disjunctive acceptance decision rule:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{Accept}(s' \mid s, T) \iff \; &\Big( p_t \ge \tau_{\text{acc}}(t) \Big) \\
  &\lor\; \Big( P_{\text{SA}}(\Delta C, T) > U \Big)
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:lac_decision_rule}
\end{equation}
where $p_t = \phi_{\text{LAC}}(\mathbf{z}_t)$, $U \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0, 1)$, and $\tau_{\text{acc}}(t) \in [0.40, 0.75]$ is an adaptive confidence threshold. 

The disjunctive operator ($\lor$) makes LAC an acceptance booster / rescue mechanism with two properties: (i) Preservation of SA-Approved Moves: any consulted move that standard Simulated Annealing would accept ($P_{\text{SA}} > U$) is also accepted by rule~\eqref{eq:lac_decision_rule}; and (ii) Predictive Rescue Transitions: when SA would reject a non-improving move ($P_{\text{SA}} \le U$), the move is still accepted if the classifier predicts a downstream incumbent improvement with probability $p_t \ge \tau_{\text{acc}}(t)$. Property (ii) adds transitions that SA rejects, so rule~\eqref{eq:lac_decision_rule} defines a different Markov transition kernel from SA, and the classical asymptotic convergence results for SA under logarithmic cooling~\cite{hajek1988cooling} do not carry over. We make no convergence claim for the modified kernel: the search uses geometric cooling within a finite budget, and the contribution of LAC is assessed empirically (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}). The supervision target is observational. The first $W_{\text{lac}} = 300$ consulted decisions use pure SA while the classifier collects training data; for every consulted move, including warm-up moves, the label records whether the global best travel distance improved within the next $H_{\text{lac}} = 80$ LAC decisions (not 80 ALNS iterations), irrespective of whether the move was accepted. The label therefore indicates a promising search context rather than the causal effect of accepting that particular move.

Because decision rule~\eqref{eq:lac_decision_rule} operates as an additive booster, it systematically increases the volume of accepted non-improving transitions during deep cooling. In our ablation and limitations analyses (Sections~\ref{sec:ablation_results} and~\ref{sec:disc_limitations}), we discuss the methodological implication of distinguishing state-conditioned lookahead intelligence from generic acceptance rate inflation.

\subsubsection{Feature Representation and Endogenous Training Dynamics}
The LAC input feature vector $\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^9$ comprises 9 normalized features:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{z}_t = \Big[
    &\frac{\Delta C}{|C_{\text{cur}}|}, \; \frac{T}{T_0}, \; \frac{\text{no\_imp}}{\text{patience}}, \nonumber \\
    &\operatorname{clip}(\Delta NV, -2, 2), \; \min\Big(\frac{t}{10000}, 1\Big), \nonumber \\
    &\tau_{\text{tight}}, \; \rho_{\text{fill}}, \; \bar{s}, \; \exp\left(-\frac{\max(\Delta C, 0)}{T}\right) \Big]
  \label{eq:lac_features}
\end{align}
During the first 300 consulted decisions, the solver uses Simulated Annealing alone while queuing the features of each decision. After a further $H_{\text{lac}} = 80$ consulted decisions, each queued decision receives a binary label: $y_t = 1$ if the best distance $C_{\text{best}}$ has decreased since that decision, and $0$ otherwise. The label therefore follows distance, not the lexicographic objective, so a fleet reduction that raises the best distance receives $y_t = 0$. The threshold rises with progress $p = \min(t/10000, 1)$ as $\tau_{\text{acc}}(t) = 0.40 + 0.35\,p^{0.75}$.

Because training examples are labeled online from the active search trajectory, the empirical training distribution is endogenous and non-stationary. Every 20 consulted decisions, LAC trains a 4-layer MLP ($9 \to 64 \to 48 \to 32 \to 1$, with LayerNorm and $0.1$ dropout after the first layer) on a batch of 64 from a buffer of 5,000 labeled decisions, using Adam ($\text{lr} = 10^{-3}$) with class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{LAC}}(\phi) = -\frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^B \Big[ w_1 y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - p_i) \Big]
  \label{eq:lac_loss}
\end{equation}
where $p_i = \phi_{\text{LAC}}(\mathbf{z}_i)$ and $w_1 = N_{\text{neg}} / N_{\text{pos}}$ balances positive and negative labels within the batch.

\subsection{Heuristic Search Components}
\label{sec:search_muscle}

The low-level search combines cached insertion checks, granular candidate lists, a frozen GNN heatmap and ejection chains.

\subsubsection{Constant-Time Insertion Feasibility Queries via Savelsbergh Forward Slack}
To eliminate $O(m)$ trajectory recalculation during candidate move evaluations, we maintain Savelsbergh forward time slack $F_i$ for each node $i$ along route $R = (v_0, v_1, \dots, v_m)$ \cite{savelsbergh1992general}:
\begin{equation}
  F_i = \min_{i \le j \le m} \left\{ l_j - w_j + \sum_{k=i+1}^{j} W_k \right\}
  \label{eq:forward_slack}
\end{equation}
A candidate insertion of customer $u$ between positions $i$ and $i+1$ inducing arrival delay $\Delta t_u$ at node $i+1$ preserves temporal feasibility for all subsequent customers if and only if $\max(0, \Delta t_u - W_{i+1}) \le F_{i+1}$, where $W_k$ is the waiting time at node $k$, given that the prefix up to $v_i$ is feasible and $u$ is served within its window; capacity feasibility additionally requires $q(R) + q_u \le Q$. Given a cached timing profile of the route (arrival times, load, and forward slacks or, equivalently, the latest feasible service start of each node, which our implementation stores), each such query costs $O(1)$. The arrival time delay shifted to customer $v_{i+1}$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t_u = \max\bigl(0, e_u - (w_i + s_i + t_{i, u})\bigr) + t_{i, u} + s_u + t_{u, i+1} - t_{i, i+1}
  \label{eq:delta_t_insertion}
\end{equation}
where $w_i$ is the service start time at node $i$, $s$ denotes service durations, and $e_u$ is earliest ready time. The $O(1)$ bound applies to a single insertion query and holds only while the cached profile is valid. Building or refreshing the profile costs $O(m)$ for a route of length $m$ and is required after every accepted modification of that route; scanning all $m+1$ insertion positions of one customer is therefore $O(m)$ per route, and evaluating one customer against $K$ routes is $O(Km)$ before granular pruning. Moves that reverse or relocate a segment (2-opt, ejection chains) recompute arrival times along the modified segment, so their cost grows with the segment length, and only the suffix check after the segment uses the cached slack. We claim no $O(1)$ bound for complete moves or iterations.

\subsubsection{Granular Correlated Neighborhood Search (GNS)}
Following granular search principles \cite{toth2003granular}, inter-route operators restrict candidate evaluations to the $k=15$ nearest customers of each customer by travel distance, precomputed once per instance with the depot excluded. This bounds the candidate insertion search space to $O(k N)$ move queries per iteration.

\subsubsection{Lightweight Graph Neural Network for Spatial Edge Guidance}
\label{sec:gnn_guidance}
To complement heuristic candidate lists with learned topological priors, a lightweight Graph Neural Network (GNN) evaluates a spatial edge probability heatmap $\mathbf{H} \in [0, 1]^{(N+1)\times (N+1)}$. The graph encodes $N+1$ nodes (depot + customers) with 6-dimensional normalized node features $\mathbf{v}_i \in \mathbb{R}^6$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_i = \left[ \tilde{x}_i, \; \tilde{y}_i, \; \frac{q_i}{Q}, \; \frac{e_i}{T_{\text{depot}}}, \; \frac{l_i}{T_{\text{depot}}}, \; \frac{s_i}{T_{\text{depot}}} \right]
  \label{eq:gnn_node_feats}
\end{equation}
where $(\tilde{x}_i, \tilde{y}_i) \in [0, 1]^2$ are min-max normalized Cartesian coordinates, $q_i / Q$ is demand normalized by vehicle capacity, and $(e_i, l_i, s_i)$ are ready time, due time, and service duration normalized by the depot operational horizon $T_{\text{depot}}$. Message passing runs over sparse $k$-nearest-neighbor edge sets ($K=16$, with slot 0 pinned to the depot) with 1-dimensional edge feature $e_{ij} = c_{ij} / c_{\max}$ representing normalized Euclidean distance.

The network architecture comprises a linear embedding layer ($d_h = 64$), 3 sparse joint node-edge message-passing layers with LayerNorm and residual connections, and a bilinear projection head:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{H}_{ij} = \sigma\left( \mathbf{z}_i^{\top} \mathbf{W}_{\text{edge}} \mathbf{z}_j \right)
  \label{eq:gnn_bilinear_head}
\end{equation}
producing edge selection probabilities $\mathbf{H}_{ij} \in [0, 1]$. Message passing uses the 16 nearest neighbors of each node. The registered checkpoint was trained offline with a weighted binary cross-entropy loss and sampled negative edges on 48 synthetic instances (16 each of the C, R and RC distributions, half with $N=100$ and half with $N=200$), with 12 further synthetic validation instances of the same sizes. Training targets are the route edges of a 10-second ALNS run without the GNN, so they are heuristic labels and not optimal edges. The checkpoint was selected by minimum validation loss (epoch 71 of 150, Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$, one training seed), and no benchmark test instance was used for training or selection. On the 12 validation instances, thresholds of 0.05, 0.01 and 0.003 retain $23.3\%$, $29.6\%$ and $34.1\%$ of candidate arcs and $99.45\%$, $99.90\%$ and $99.90\%$ of teacher edges. These instances also selected the checkpoint, so the figures are optimistic, and they measure agreement with heuristic labels, not a routing gain. The effect of the GNN on solutions is estimated only by the factorial ablation. $N=400$ lies outside the training sizes, so use at that size is an extrapolation. During search, network weights are frozen, and inference is executed once per instance during preprocessing in $O(N K)$ time, populating a static heatmap $\mathbf{H}$ that guides repair and filters insertion positions.

\subsubsection{Generalized Ejection Chains (GEC)}
\label{sec:gec_methodology}
To attack vehicle-count plateaus, a 3-level ejection chain tries to eliminate the shortest route. It is attempted at every iteration in mode $a_5$, every fourth iteration when a route has at most four customers, and every twentieth consecutive iteration without improvement. Customers of the target route are processed by decreasing demand, and each displaced customer $u$ triggers a search over all other routes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[(i)] Level 1 (Direct Insertion): insert $u$ into another route $R_j$ at minimum added distance;
    \item[(ii)] Level 2 (Single-Customer Displacement): eject $v \in R_j$, insert $u$ into $R_j$, and insert $v$ into a third route $R_k$; and
    \item[(iii)] Level 3 (Two-Customer Chained Displacement): eject $v \in R_j$ and insert $u$; eject $w \in R_k$ and insert $v$; and insert $w$ into a further route. This level runs only when at most 30 routes remain.
\end{itemize}
The search has no candidate beam and no internal time check, so the solver can stop it only after it returns, while the supervisor enforces the cutoff independently. Each feasibility test replays the modified route at linear cost, not the $O(1)$ cached query above. For $m$ displaced customers, $K$ routes and maximum route length $L$, the worst-case work is $O(m K^3 L^4)$ (Supplementary Section~3).

\subsubsection{Feasibility Invariant Across Operational Layers}
\label{sec:feasibility_invariant}
Feasibility differs by operational level: (1) Current solution ($s_{\text{cur}}$): may be infeasible under penalized acceptance (Section~\ref{sec:search_phases}); (2) Candidate moves ($s_{\text{cand}}$): violations are penalized in those phases and rejected otherwise; (3) Best solution ($s_{\text{inc}}$): only solutions with full capacity and time-window compliance can update it; (4) Elite archive and (5) Route pool: hold only feasible solutions and routes; and (6) Returned solution ($s^*$): counted only after the supervisor validates it in the original problem space (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\subsection{Matheuristic Recombination: Time-Limited HiGHS Set Partitioning}
\label{sec:set_partitioning}

During search stagnation, elite feasible routes accumulated in Route Pool $\mathcal{P}$ are recombined by solving a time-limited Set Partitioning integer linear programming formulation:
\begin{align}
  \min \quad &\sum_{r \in \mathcal{P}} \left( \lambda_{\text{pen}} \bar{c} + c_r \right) y_r \label{eq:sp_obj} \\
  \text{s.t.} \quad &\sum_{r \in \mathcal{P} : i \in r} y_r = 1, \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{N} \label{eq:sp_cover} \\
  &y_r \in \{0, 1\}, \quad \forall r \in \mathcal{P} \label{eq:sp_binary}
\end{align}
where $c_r$ is route travel distance, $\bar{c}$ is pool mean route distance, and $\lambda_{\text{pen}} \in \{2.0, 5.0, 12.0, 30.0, 50.0\}$ is a penalty multiplier; the vehicle penalty $\lambda_{\text{pen}}\bar{c}$ has a floor of 200 in the implementation. At most 800 columns of the pool enter one model, and a vehicle ceiling constraint may be added. If exact partitioning fails, the solver falls back to a set-covering relaxation with duplicate customers removed afterwards. 

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent Proposition 1 (Penalty Bound for Lexicographic Fleet Preservation): \textit{Let $\mathcal{Y}_{\text{SP}} \subseteq \{0, 1\}^{|\mathcal{P}|}$ be the set of feasible partitions over route pool $\mathcal{P}$. If $\lambda_{\text{pen}} \bar{c} > \sup_{\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2 \in \mathcal{Y}_{\text{SP}}} \left( \sum_{r \in \mathbf{y}_1} c_r - \sum_{r \in \mathbf{y}_2} c_r \right)$, any partition $\mathbf{y}_1$ with $\sum_{r} y_{1, r} < \sum_{r} y_{2, r}$ strictly achieves a lower objective value than $\mathbf{y}_2$, guaranteeing exact lexicographic fleet minimization priority over distance within the column subset $\mathcal{P}$.}
\vspace{1mm}

In practice, with Route Pool $\mathcal{P}$ bounded to $|\mathcal{P}| \le 2000$ high-quality elite columns, route substitutions between valid set partitions yield cumulative distance deltas that are bounded by $K_{\max} \cdot c_{\max}^{\text{route}}$. To guarantee certified lexicographic preservation even at extreme scales ($N=400$), the solver applies the certified threshold $\lambda_{\text{pen}}^{\text{cert}} = \max\bigl(\lambda_{\text{pen}}\bar{c}, \; (1 + \epsilon_{\text{margin}}) K_{\max} c_{\max}^{\text{route}}\bigr)$ with clearance margin $\epsilon_{\text{margin}} = 0.20$ at the largest scale of the multi-scale penalty grid $\lambda_{\text{pen}} \in \{2.0, 5.0, 12.0, 30.0, 50.0\}\bar{c}$ (formal derivation in Supplementary Material Proposition~1). The smaller scales are tried first and do not, in general, satisfy the condition of Proposition~1, so the partition they return need not be lexicographically optimal within $\mathcal{P}$; every partition is, however, subject to the hard fleet ceiling of the query. Proposition~1 therefore concerns lexicographic ordering among partitions of the route pool at the certified scale only. It does not imply optimality of the partition for the original VRPTW, whose optimal routes need not be in $\mathcal{P}$.

Across the whole search, the hierarchical objective is enforced at the level of the incumbent rather than at every transition. The current solution may temporarily accept a one-vehicle increase in the diversification mode, and penalty-relaxed phases may visit infeasible states. However, a candidate replaces the best solution only if it has fewer vehicles, or the same number of vehicles and strictly lower distance, after independent validation of exact cover, capacity and time windows in the original instance. Consequently the reported solution is non-increasing in $\NV$ over time and never trades a vehicle for distance, but no claim is made that every intermediate move respects the ordering.

\subsubsection{Route Pool Deduplication and Capacity Management}
To maintain diverse yet non-redundant columns: (1) Frozenset Cover Deduplication: routes visiting identical customer sets $\text{cover}(R) = \{v_1, \dots, v_m\}$ are deduplicated via hash tables, retaining only the minimal-distance tour permutation; and (2) Dual-Slot Pruning: pool capacity $|\mathcal{P}|_{\max} = \min(2000, 600 + 4N)$ is partitioned into Slot A (75\% capacity, sorting by cost-per-stop $c_r / |r|$) and Slot B (25\% capacity, preserving longest sub-routes $|r|$ to supply high-occupancy columns for fleet reduction), with at most $M_{\text{cust}} = \min(60, 28 + \lfloor N/10 \rfloor)$ routes per customer.
Branch-and-cut solves are executed via HiGHS~\cite{huangfu2018parallelizing}. Each penalty scale is one query, limited to $\max(1, \min(30, 4+0.05N)/3)$\,s (3, 4.7 and 8\,s at $N=100$, 200 and 400) and capped by the remaining budget.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Tri-Level Hybrid DDQN-ALNS Overview}
\label{alg:hybrid_ddqn_alns}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Instance $\mathcal{I}$, Hyperparameters $\Theta$, Time budget $T$
\Ensure Best feasible routing plan $s^*$
\State $s^* \leftarrow s_{\text{inc}} \leftarrow \text{GreedyInit}(\mathcal{I})$; $\mathcal{P} \leftarrow \text{ExtractRoutes}(s^*)$
\State Initialize Macro DDQN $Q_{\text{macro}}$, Micro DDQN $Q_{\text{micro}}$, LAC $\phi_{\text{LAC}}$
\For{$t = 1, 2, \dots$ until $80\%$ of $T$ has elapsed}
    \If{$t \bmod \Delta_{\text{seg}} == 1$} \Comment{Macro Tier: SMDP regime selection}
        \State Update regime $a_k \leftarrow \pi_{\text{macro}}(\mathbf{s}_k^{\text{macro}})$; trigger GEC/HiGHS if selected
    \EndIf
    \State Select $(d, r) \leftarrow \pi_{\text{micro}}(\mathbf{s}_t^{\text{micro}})$ via confidence gating~\eqref{eq:q_eff_blending}
    \State $s_{\text{cand}} \leftarrow \text{GranularLocalSearch}(\text{Repair}(\text{Destroy}(s_{\text{cur}}, d), r), k=15)$
    \State Accept $s_{\text{cand}}$ by the phase's rule: penalized SA, or SA or LAC ($\phi_{\text{LAC}}$)
    \State Update route pool $\mathcal{P}$, train DDQNs and LAC on mature experiences
\EndFor
\State Run tail phases until $T$: recombination, route elimination, polishing
\State \Return $s^*$ \Comment{Certified by the supervisor}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Guided Ejection Search Phase}
\label{sec:ges}
An optional phase runs the route-minimization heuristic of Nagata and Br{\"a}ysy~\cite{nagata2009ges} once, before the main search, for $10\%$ of the remaining budget. It removes a random route and places its customers in an ejection pool. It then takes customers from the pool one at a time and inserts each one if a feasible insertion exists. Otherwise it increments the customer's penalty counter, applies the feasible ejection of at most $k_{\max}=5$ customers from one route that has the smallest penalty sum, and perturbs the solution with 100 random feasible relocate, exchange and 2-opt* moves. A route is eliminated when the pool empties, and the attempt restarts after 20,000 steps otherwise. The phase ends at its time limit or at the capacity bound $\lceil \sum_i d_i / Q \rceil$, and it reads no reference fleet. If it reduces the fleet, the result receives one local-search pass before the main search. This is a classical method, so any gain from it is not a gain from learning.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the method on 74 standard benchmark instances: Solomon-100 (56 instances across 6 families)~\cite{Solomon1987}, Gehring-Homberger 200-customer (12 instances) and Gehring-Homberger 400-customer (6 instances)~\cite{gehring1999parallel}. Five questions structure the evaluation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item RQ1 (Comparison at equal compute): At 300\,s, how does the method compare with ALNS-Base, PyVRP-ILS and adapted NeuroLKH on fleet size and, at matched fleet size, on distance?
    \item RQ2 (Budget): How do these comparisons change at 10, 60 and 900\,s?
    \item RQ3 (Components): Which components change results, and does the learning bundle add to the heuristic bundle?
    \item RQ4 (Scale): How do the comparisons differ at 100, 200 and 400 customers?
    \item RQ5 (Families): How do they differ across clustered, random and mixed instance families? This question is descriptive.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Setup, Baseline Fairness, and Cold-Start Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

All experiments ran on one Apple M1 notebook (4 performance and 4 efficiency cores, 16\,GB memory, macOS 26.5.2), with four cells at a time and one numerical-library thread per cell. macOS assigns cores itself, so processes were not pinned to cores.

\textit{Machine-speed variation.} A wall-clock budget does not fix the compute a run receives. PyVRP and NeuroLKH with the same seed retrace the same search, so the time needed to reach the same (fleet, distance) step measures machine speed. On 18 instances at 60\,s, cells of our first full sweep took a median of 2.3 times as long as the same cells on a later night, ranging from about 0.65 to 9.6 times per instance. During that sweep the notebook was also used for other work and was at times unplugged or asleep, but the cause of the slowdown is not established. Absolute times therefore do not describe a steady machine speed, and comparisons are valid within a block of cells that ran together, not across blocks. In the reported rerun, all cases of one instance, seed and cutoff run together as a block. A cell whose wall-clock span exceeds its monotonic duration by more than 5\,s was frozen by machine sleep, and its whole block runs again. Each cell records power state and load at its start, and the primary comparisons are repeated without blocks that started a cell at a one-minute load above 8. Other work on the notebook raised the load as high as 233 early in the rerun. Before any result was read, we therefore moved 58 of the first 111 blocks at 300\,s, those with a cell started above load 8, aside unread and ran them again, and from then on no new cell started above that load. A cell can still start below that load and then run above it, so also before any result was read, we began sampling load once a minute and added two repeats at 300\,s: one without blocks that have a cell whose mean load during its run was above 8, and one that also leaves out blocks with a cell not measured this way. Load during a run is not known for the roughly 450 cells at 300\,s that finished before sampling began.

ALNS-Base is our own implementation in the framework of Ropke and Pisinger~\cite{Ropke2006, pisinger2007general}, not a reproduction of it. It shares the 13 destroy and 5 repair operators of our search infrastructure. It loads no GNN, so its two heatmap-guided destroy operators receive no heatmap. Pairs are selected by Thompson sampling over 65 Beta posteriors instead of a roulette wheel, with scores $(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3) = (33, 9, 3)$ normalized by $\sigma_1$, posterior decay $0.95$ every 100 iterations, and a posterior reset after 1,250 iterations without improvement. Simulated Annealing starts at $T_0 = 0.05\,c_0 / \ln 2$, where $c_0$ is the cost of the starting solution, and cools geometrically with $\alpha = 0.99975$. Its main loop applies no local search, ejection chains or set partitioning. Initialization is algorithm-specific: ALNS-Base starts from the unweighted greedy construction (\textsf{\small BuildGreedyInitialSolution}), the hybrid starts from the same construction with GNN-weighted insertion costs, and PyVRP-ILS and NeuroLKH use their own construction procedures; construction and model inference are charged to each solver's budget. A defect in the greedy construction, which did not check the depot return deadline, made the ALNS-Base start infeasible on R1\_2\_5, so all 20 ALNS-Base runs on that instance terminated without a solution. We corrected the construction, which leaves the start unchanged on the other 73 instances, and reran those 20 runs.

PyVRP-ILS is the iterated local search of PyVRP~\cite{wouda2024pyvrp}, version 0.14.1, with a vehicle fixed cost set above any possible travel cost so that its objective follows the fleet-first order. PyVRP's HGS is not the method used here. NeuroLKH-adapted runs the pretrained CVRPTW checkpoint and LKH binary of NeuroLKH~\cite{xin2021neurolkh} at a pinned upstream commit, with cold restarts at decreasing fleet sizes. It is an adaptation, not a replication of the authors' experiments, and we did not retrain it or tune it on benchmark instances. Both baselines work on integer-scaled models, so every candidate is mapped back and certified in the original problem space.

All cases share one supervisor. A case's clock starts in a fresh process with an empty work directory and JIT cache, and construction, model inference, search, JIT compilation after the start, certification and communication with the supervisor are charged to its budget. Imports and loading of frozen models are recorded separately. An incumbent counts only if the supervisor has received, validated and hashed it before the cutoff, and the process must return within $[T-0.05, T+0.5]$\,s of the cutoff $T$.

\paragraph{Reference-Fleet Information}
In the first full sweep (Section~\ref{sec:first_sweep}), the hybrid read the best-known fleet size $\NVs{BKS}$~\cite{sintef_homberger} of each benchmark instance during search. It served as the floor at which vehicle-reduction phases stop, as the trigger for switching set partitioning to a distance-only objective, and as a parameter of the recombination and route-elimination steps. None of the baselines uses this information, it is not available for new instances, and equal compute alone does not remove this asymmetry. The rerun therefore disables every lookup. With the Guided Ejection Search phase on, the capacity bound takes the place of the reference fleet; with it off, the hybrid uses its fallbacks for unknown instances. The effect of reading the reference fleet was not re-estimated under matched conditions, so we report only that the first sweep's hybrid read it.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter Specification and Experimental Firewall}
The hyperparameters in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters} combine literature defaults (for example the Adam optimizer parameters and the Polyak update rate $\tau=0.005$) with values set by hand during development. We did not retain a calibration log, so we cannot document how the hand-set values were chosen or show that they are independent of earlier runs on the benchmark instances. They were frozen before the sweeps reported here. One solver rule reads family information from the benchmark's naming convention: the fleet-reduction search receives more iterations when an instance name begins with RC and the current fleet exceeds the target by at least one vehicle.

The test set did not, however, remain untouched by development. After the first full sweep, per-family losses on the test instances led us to evaluate a Guided Ejection Search phase before the main search. This is a classical method, so any gain from it is not a gain from learning. Its parameters were chosen only on 30 development instances with different instance identities: 18 are 100-customer subsamples of Gehring-Homberger instances, and 12 are unmodified Gehring-Homberger instances outside the test set. They are not independent of the test set, because they share customer coordinates with test instances of the same families and up to $81\%$ of a development instance's customer records (coordinates, demand, time window and service time) are identical to those of a test instance. The rule for adopting the phase was fixed before a single contemporaneous rerun on the test set, and that rerun was not used to change the solver.

\textit{Training and testing protocol.} The Macro DDQN, Micro DDQN and LAC have no offline training phase. They start from random weights in every run and learn online within that run's time budget, so they have no train/test split, nothing learned in one run is carried to another, and we make no claim that a learned policy transfers between instances. Only the GNN is trained offline. It is then frozen and uses only the synthetic data described in Section~\ref{sec:gnn_guidance}. Its generalization is limited to what the factorial ablation measures on the benchmark instances.

To eliminate experimental contamination and guarantee reproducibility, the protocol enforces four principles:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Strict Independent Cold-Starts: every individual solver execution is conducted within an isolated ephemeral directory (\textsf{\small ColdStartScope}), purged immediately upon task completion, with all neural networks (Macro DDQN, Micro DDQN, LAC) initialized with random weights from scratch at the beginning of each instance run;
    \item Deterministic RNG Seeding: each worker process initializes isolated random seeds across Python's \textsf{\small random}, \textsf{\small numpy}, and PyTorch (\textsf{\small torch.manual\_seed} and \textsf{\small torch.cuda.manual\_seed\_all}) across 5 independent seeds ($\{42, 43, 44, 45, 46\}$); 
    \item Objective Hierarchy: fleet size is the primary endpoint, and distance is compared only between seed pairs with matched fleet size (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}); and
    \item Machine-Verifiable Configuration: all solver executions output JSON metadata containing \textsf{\small resolved\_components} to explicitly verify active modules at runtime.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:statistics}
Estimands and intervals follow a design fixed before the sweeps. The unit is a seed pair within an instance, not a best run or an instance mean. For each pair, the \emph{fleet score} is $+1$ if the proposed configuration uses fewer vehicles, $-1$ if it uses more, and $0$ if they tie; a case with a solution beats a case without one, and two cases without a solution tie. Scores are averaged over seeds and instances within each size and combined with fixed weights of $56/74$, $12/74$ and $6/74$. The score therefore lies in $[-1, 1]$ and is the net share of seed pairs won. It is not a difference in mean vehicles.

The \emph{matched distance gap} is the percentage distance difference relative to the comparator, computed only on seed pairs with equal fleet size and averaged in the same way. Negative values favor the proposed configuration. It is conditional on matched fleets and is not an unconditional treatment effect.

Uncertainty comes from a percentile bootstrap (10,000 replicates, fixed seed) that resamples the five seeds and, within each size, the four groups of instances that share customer geometry, so that instances with the same coordinates move together. With four groups per size the intervals are coarse, and several have an endpoint at exactly zero. An interval is reported as undefined when any replicate has no matched pair in a size stratum. The three primary comparisons at 300\,s use Bonferroni-adjusted $98.33\%$ intervals, and the eight primary factorial contrasts at 300\,s use $99.38\%$ intervals. Other budgets, size strata and exploratory contrasts use unadjusted $95\%$ intervals and are descriptive.

A missing cell caused by infrastructure failure is not an algorithm outcome, so the primary treatment drops that seed pair from that comparison only, and we report bounds that count such gaps as failures. A cell in which the algorithm itself found no solution stays in the analysis and loses its pair.

\subsection{Managerial Implications for Commercial Logistics}
\label{sec:disc_managerial}

In commercial freight operations, capital depreciation, vehicle financing, insurance, and driver shift wages dominate marginal fuel consumption \cite{Toth2014, braysy2005vehicle}. Fixed vehicle overhead represents the primary cost driver. Consequently, eliminating a single vehicle yields substantial recurring savings that surpass marginal distance improvements. Extra compute is therefore worth spending when it removes a vehicle, but the solver that removes it need not be this one. \PENDING{say which solver the results favor at planning budgets of 5 and 15 minutes.}

\subsection{Analytical Properties and Architectural Design Principles}
\label{sec:disc_theory}
Related derivations are in Supplementary Sections~1--4, and the full algorithm is in Supplementary Section~5.

Property 1 (Lexicographic Consistency): \textit{Under scalar surrogate $F(s) = \mathcal{M} \cdot \NV(s) + \TD(s)$ with $\mathcal{M} > \max_{s} \TD(s) - \min_{s} \TD(s)$, $F(s_1) < F(s_2) \iff s_1 \prec s_2$.} With $\mathcal{M} = 10^5$ the condition holds for all evaluated instances, whose travel distances are below $10^5$; the property concerns the comparison of evaluated solutions and implies no optimality guarantee.

Property 2 (Granular Candidate Space Reduction): \textit{Restricting each customer to its $k=15$ nearest customers reduces the candidate arc space from $N(N-1)$ to $kN$ arcs, a reduction of $\Gamma_{\text{prune}}(N) = 1 - k/(N-1)$.} This removes $84.85\%$, $92.46\%$ and $96.24\%$ of customer arcs at $N=100$, $200$ and $400$. This is arithmetic on the candidate lists and is separate from the GNN thresholds in Section~\ref{sec:gnn_guidance}.

Design Principle 1 (Multi-Scale Penalty Grid): \textit{In Set Partitioning over Route Pool $\mathcal{P}$, geometric penalty schedule $\lambda_{\text{pen}} \in \{2.0, \dots, 50.0\}\bar{c}$ systematically shifts from distance preservation to vehicle consolidation.} At $\lambda_{\text{pen}}=50.0$, the vehicle penalty strictly dominates partition distance variance, enforcing fleet minimisation during HiGHS solves~\cite{huangfu2018parallelizing}.

Design Principle 2 (Downstream Lookahead via LAC): \textit{Unlike Simulated Annealing where non-improving acceptance vanishes ($P_{\text{SA}} \to 0$ as $T \to 0$), LAC evaluates structural transition promise using a delayed observational label ($H_{\text{lac}} = 80$ LAC decisions).} Retaining moves with predicted probability $p_t \ge \tau_{\text{acc}}$ is intended to aid plateau escape; this is an empirical design choice, not a convergence guarantee (Section~\ref{sec:lac}).

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}
The main limitations are these. (i) Benchmark Scope: the evaluation covers 74 instances with four geometry groups per size and few large instances, so it says little about other distributions. (ii) MIP Dependency: Set Partitioning relies on HiGHS throughput within a per-query time limit. (iii) Compute Overhead: online neural updates and Set Partitioning consume part of each wall-clock budget that would otherwise go to search iterations. (iv) Static Scope: the framework targets static VRPTW. (v) GNN Scale Shift: the GNN was trained and validated with one training seed at $N=100$ and $200$ only, so use at $N=400$ is an extrapolation, and its validation recall is measured against heuristic labels. (vi) LAC Disentanglement: because LAC operates disjunctively ($\text{Accept} \iff p_t \ge \tau_{\text{acc}} \lor P_{\text{SA}} > U$), future work is needed to isolate predictive lookahead from generic acceptance volume. (vii) Effective Compute: machine speed varied between blocks of cells (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), so results are comparable within a block and do not describe a fixed amount of compute. (viii) One Machine and Fixed Baseline Settings: all runs used one notebook, and the baselines were not tuned for these budgets. (ix) Test-Informed Development: test results motivated the Guided Ejection Search phase, and the development instances share coordinates with test instances (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Every incumbent counted in the results is validated in the original problem space by the supervisor before it counts, and cells without a valid incumbent are reported as unavailable.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented Tri-Level Hybrid DDQN-ALNS, a hierarchical learning-augmented optimization framework for the VRPTW. It separates macro plateau governance (Dueling DDQN SMDP) from micro operator selection (Dueling DDQN with Softmax-Entropy policy gating) and predictive lookahead acceptance (LAC). Feasibility rests on insertion checks against cached route profiles during search and on validation in the original problem space before any solution counts.
